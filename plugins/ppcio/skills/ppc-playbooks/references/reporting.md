# Reporting & Communication Knowledge Foundation

> Reporting in PPC is a trust-building discipline that translates performance data into business narratives. The report's job is not to display numbers -- it is to make the reader feel informed, confident in the strategy, and clear on what happens next. A report that catalogues metrics without interpretation is a spreadsheet with a header, not a strategic deliverable.

## First Principles

**Every number needs two comparisons or it means nothing.** "$5,000 spent" is noise. "$5,000 spent, down 8% from last month, generating 15% more leads at $12 below target CPA" is a story. The two comparisons are always: versus the prior period (is this better or worse than before?) and versus the target (is this meeting the goal?). Without both, the reader cannot evaluate whether a number is good, bad, or irrelevant. This applies to every metric in every report format -- monthly, weekly, quarterly, or escalation. No naked numbers.

**Hiding problems destroys trust faster than the problems themselves.** Clients who discover a performance issue you did not mention will question every insight you did provide. The instinct is to lead with wins and minimize challenges, but the discipline is to present both with equal honesty. The structure is always: acknowledge the problem, explain the root cause (own what you control), quantify the impact in business terms, and present a specific plan. A report that buries bad news is a liability. A report that addresses challenges with a plan builds the trust that retains accounts.

**Translate every metric into business language or cut it.** The client hired you to grow their business, not to teach them Google Ads terminology. "CPA decreased 15%" is a PPC metric. "Cost per new customer dropped 15% -- at current volume that saves $3,600 per quarter" is a business finding. Five translations that must happen every time: CPA becomes "cost per new customer," CTR becomes "click rate," impression share becomes "visibility," conversion value becomes "revenue from ads," Quality Score becomes "ad relevance rating." If a metric cannot be translated into something the client's CEO would understand, it does not belong in a client-facing document.

**Reports are written for the person who receives the forward, not the person who opens it first.** The account manager reads the report. Then they forward it to the CMO. Then the CMO shares a section with the CFO. Each forward strips context. The executive summary, the headline metrics, and the next steps must survive this forwarding chain intact. Write every section as if it will be read without the sections around it. If a finding requires reading three previous sections to understand, it is poorly written.

**Signal-to-noise ratio determines report quality, not length.** A three-section report with sharp insight beats an eight-section report that restates the same metric three different ways. Good months need shorter reports than bad months. If CPA is below target and volume is stable, two paragraphs of analysis plus next steps is sufficient. Manufacturing additional "insights" to fill a template on a quiet month signals disengagement with the data, not thoroughness.

## The Decision Landscape

**Report format must match the communication need.** A monthly report is a comprehensive performance narrative with executive summary, campaign detail, and strategic next steps -- the reader should finish feeling they understand account health completely. A weekly email is a 150-200 word pulse check -- what happened, what you are doing, any flags needing input. A monthly summary email is a digestible alternative to the full report for clients who prefer brevity -- key metrics, one win, one concern, priorities ahead. An escalation email addresses something that cannot wait for the regular cycle -- direct, evidence-based, with a specific ask and deadline. QBR prep is structured ammunition for a quarterly conversation -- organized by theme, not by month, connecting PPC metrics to business outcomes. Choosing the wrong format for the situation wastes the reader's time and misses the communication goal.

**The executive summary is the entire report in miniature.** Two to three sentences that a CEO could read and immediately understand account health. It is not a teaser that encourages reading more -- it is the whole story compressed. Include the verdict (on track, ahead, or behind), the biggest risk, and the highest-leverage action. If the reader stops after the executive summary, they should still have the essential information. This applies to monthly reports, QBR prep, and monthly summary emails alike. Weekly emails accomplish the same goal with their opening sentence.

**Metric presentation precision must match data confidence.** With 30 or more conversions, use point estimates: "CPA was $42, 16% below the $50 target." With 10 to 30 conversions, use whole numbers and directional language: "CPA trending around $45, below target, with moderate confidence." With fewer than 10 conversions, use ranges: "Cost per lead between $80 and $120 based on limited data." Presenting a point estimate of $93.47 CPA based on 6 conversions implies precision that does not exist. The sample size determines the language, and the language must communicate the uncertainty honestly.

**Performance scenarios determine narrative tone and structure.** When CPA is 20%+ below target with volume up, the tone is confident but not arrogant -- lead with what enabled the results and how to sustain them. When targets are met with steady improvement, the tone is professional satisfaction -- lead with the trajectory and what is compounding. When targets are met but growth is flat, the tone is pragmatic honesty -- lead with what needs to change for growth. When targets are missed by 10-30%, the tone is direct and solutions-oriented -- acknowledge reality, explain root cause, present the action plan. When targets are missed by 30%+, the tone is serious and accountable -- full transparency with a specific recovery plan. Matching tone to scenario prevents the two trust-destroying failures: celebrating mediocrity and sugarcoating problems.

**Every challenge section must end with a plan.** "Non-brand CPA rose to $38, 12% above target" is a problem statement. "Non-brand CPA rose to $38, 12% above target, driven by increased competition on your top 5 keywords. We are testing 3 new ad angles this month to improve click rate and reduce cost" is how a trusted advisor communicates. A challenge without a plan creates anxiety. A challenge with a plan demonstrates competence. This rule has no exceptions regardless of report format -- even escalation emails end with a specific recommended action and timeline.

## Industry Branches

### Ecommerce

Ecommerce reporting centers on revenue, ROAS, and product-level performance. The primary metric the client cares about is revenue from ads and return on ad spend, not lead volume or cost per acquisition. Campaign-level detail should separate brand search (which captures existing demand and always looks strong) from non-brand search (which drives new customer acquisition and is the true growth metric) from Shopping and Performance Max (which operate at the product level). The most common reporting mistake in ecommerce is letting brand ROAS inflate the blended number, making non-brand performance look acceptable when it is actually underperforming. Always separate brand from non-brand in the performance summary. Product-level reporting adds a dimension unique to ecommerce: which products drive revenue, which consume budget without converting, and whether the Shopping feed is competitive. Average order value shifts can mask or inflate ROAS changes -- a ROAS increase driven entirely by AOV growth means the ads are not performing better, customers are just buying more expensive products.

### Lead Generation

Lead gen reporting must bridge the gap between Google Ads data and business outcomes. The platform counts form fills, but the business values qualified leads that become customers. "We generated 45 leads at $38 each" is an incomplete story if 40 of those leads were unqualified. Wherever possible, connect ad performance to downstream pipeline data -- lead-to-SQL rate, SQL-to-close rate, and revenue generated. When downstream data is unavailable, flag the limitation explicitly: "Without close rate data, we are optimizing for lead volume and cost per lead, not revenue. Providing sales outcome data would enable us to optimize for the leads most likely to close." The QBR format is especially important in lead gen because quarterly conversations are where ad performance connects to pipeline health and revenue forecasts.

### B2B SaaS

B2B SaaS reporting operates on longer time horizons and smaller data sets. Monthly reports may reference only 15-30 conversions, which limits the precision of any metric. Reporting must acknowledge this statistical reality rather than presenting thin data with false confidence. The primary metric is cost per SQL or pipeline contribution, not cost per trial signup, because trial-to-paid conversion rates vary dramatically. Attribution is challenging because the buyer journey involves multiple touchpoints over weeks or months. Reporting should include assisted conversions and view-through data when available, and should explicitly note when conversion lag may be affecting recent period numbers. QBR prep for SaaS clients should connect ad spend to pipeline value and customer acquisition cost relative to lifetime value -- the CFO question is always "is this investment generating a return that justifies continued spend?"

### Local Services

Local services reporting is driven by call volume, lead cost, and geographic performance. Many conversions happen by phone, making call tracking accuracy a prerequisite for reliable reporting. If call tracking is inconsistent or missing, lead the report with that limitation before presenting any performance data. Day-of-week and time-of-day patterns are more pronounced in local services than any other vertical -- Monday call volume may be 3x Saturday -- and weekly reports should account for these patterns rather than comparing raw day counts. Geographic reporting matters because different service areas have different competitive dynamics, demand levels, and costs per lead. Google Maps pack visibility is a performance dimension that affects paid search indirectly and should be referenced in quarterly reviews when the competitive landscape is discussed.

### High-Value Verticals (Legal, Medical, Financial)

High-value verticals generate small numbers of high-cost conversions, making every lead significant and every data point meaningful to the client. A law firm generating 8 leads per month at $400 each wants to know about each lead's quality, not just the average CPA. Reporting should include qualitative context when available -- how many leads became consultations, how many cases were signed. The cost per case signed is the metric that matters, not the cost per form fill. These verticals also face compliance requirements that affect what can be communicated in reports -- avoid making guarantees about future performance, use qualifying language for projections, and never reference specific client outcomes without permission. Monthly reports should be more detailed per lead and less focused on statistical trends, because the data volume does not support trend analysis at the campaign level.

## Diagnostic Trees

**IF all metrics are strong and nothing needs attention THEN** do not manufacture problems. Surface strategic opportunities instead: scaling headroom (impression share below 70% on profitable campaigns), new keyword territories to explore, competitive landscape shifts, or seasonal preparation. A thin report on a strong month signals disengagement. A focused report on a strong month that identifies growth opportunities demonstrates strategic value.

**IF conversion tracking appears broken (normal spend but zero conversions) THEN** lead the report with the tracking issue before reporting anything else. Do not blend periods before and after a tracking break into misleading aggregates. Delineate the before and after periods explicitly. Do not make optimization recommendations based on data from a period with broken tracking -- the only recommendation is "fix tracking."

**IF the report period has fewer than 7 days of data THEN** flag the limitation prominently. Do not present week-over-week comparisons as meaningful with partial week data. Frame the report as a snapshot rather than a trend analysis. If the client requests a report on a short period, provide it but set expectations that conclusions require at least 21 days of data to account for weekly cyclical patterns.

**IF the client is new with fewer than 30 days of data THEN** frame the report as a baseline assessment. Do not force month-over-month comparisons that do not exist. Set expectations for what next month's report will add. Benchmark against 90-day account averages or industry benchmarks rather than prior period, and explain that the first month establishes the foundation for meaningful comparison going forward.

**IF the account is PMax-only THEN** skip search term analysis entirely. Shift reporting weight to asset group performance, conversion value trends, and audience signal performance. Note that Performance Max limits visibility into what specific searches or placements drove results. If the client asks why you cannot provide search term detail, explain the PMax visibility limitation directly rather than glossing over it.

**IF CPA is above target but conversion volume increased significantly THEN** this is a trade-off that requires business judgment, not a straightforward problem. Frame it honestly: "Cost per customer rose 12% but lead volume rose 35% -- we are acquiring more customers at slightly higher cost. Net acquisition cost increased by $X per month. Whether this is the right trade-off depends on whether your priority is volume or efficiency." Never present a trade-off as either a win or a loss without acknowledging both sides.

## Mental Models

**The Revenue Translation Engine.** Every metric change must be translated into dollars. A CPA decrease of $12 multiplied by monthly conversion volume becomes quarterly savings. A conversion volume increase multiplied by average deal value becomes pipeline impact. A ROAS improvement multiplied by current spend becomes additional revenue generated per dollar invested. These translations make the "so what" explicit and connect PPC metrics to the business outcomes the reader cares about. Without revenue translation, a report is a data export. With it, the report answers the CFO question: "Is this a good investment?"

**The Forwarding Test.** Before including any section, ask: "Would the client forward this to their CEO, and would the CEO understand it without additional context?" If a section requires PPC knowledge to interpret, it needs translation or removal. If a section restates information available in the performance table, it adds no value. If a section would embarrass the client to forward (jargon-heavy, internally contradictory, or unclear), it fails the test. Every section must independently survive being extracted and shared.

**The Three-Window Comparison.** For weekly reporting, compare three windows: this week versus last week (direction), this week versus the 4-week rolling average (trend confirmation), and this week versus target (performance judgment). A 25% week-over-week spike that returns to average the following week is noise. The same spike confirmed by the 4-week trend is signal. The third window (versus target) determines whether the signal matters. This three-window approach separates real performance shifts from variance and prevents overreaction to single-week fluctuations.

**The Anomaly Triage.** Flag any metric that moved more than 20% week-over-week for investigation. Classify each flagged metric: confirmed by the 4-week trend (real signal, investigate and act), contradicted by the 4-week trend (likely noise, note and monitor), or insufficient history to determine (flag for next week). This mechanical triage prevents two opposite failures -- ignoring a genuine performance shift because it "might be noise" and panicking about normal variance because the number looked alarming in isolation.

**The Communication Mode Selector.** Performance drop or urgent issue defaults to escalation email -- direct, evidence-based, with a specific ask. Routine weekly check-in defaults to weekly email -- brief, action-oriented, under 200 words. End of month defaults to monthly report or monthly summary email depending on client preference. Quarterly review defaults to QBR prep -- themed, strategic, connecting past decisions to future direction. Choosing the wrong mode is a communication failure independent of the quality of the analysis. An escalation buried in a monthly report reaches the client too late. A routine update delivered as an escalation creates false urgency.

## Thresholds and Benchmarks

**200 words maximum** for weekly status emails. If you cannot communicate the week's performance, key actions, and flags in 200 words, you are overexplaining. The client should read a weekly email in under 60 seconds. Source: PPC.io operational standard validated across managed accounts -- emails exceeding this length show declining client engagement and response rates.

**2-3 sentences maximum** for executive summaries. This constraint forces clarity. If the executive summary cannot convey the verdict, the biggest risk, and the highest-leverage action in three sentences, the analyst has not yet identified what matters most. Source: client feedback analysis showing executives stop reading after the third sentence of any summary section.

**15% metric change** classifies a period-over-period shift as significant. Changes below 5% are noise -- do not build narrative around them. Changes between 5% and 15% are marginal -- may be real, may be variance, worth noting but not worth alarm. Changes above 15% are significant -- must be explained with root cause analysis. Source: statistical variance analysis across PPC accounts showing that sub-15% fluctuations in CPA, ROAS, and conversion volume reverse within 2-4 weeks more than 60% of the time.

**20% week-over-week movement** triggers an anomaly flag in weekly reporting. This threshold balances sensitivity (catching real problems early) against noise (not flagging normal weekly variance). Below 20%, monitor but do not act. Above 20%, investigate immediately and classify as signal or noise using the 4-week trend. Source: PPC.io weekly audit framework tuned across 100+ managed accounts.

**50+ conversions per period** provides high statistical confidence for trend analysis and metric reporting. Between 20 and 50 conversions, confidence is moderate -- note this limitation. Below 20 conversions, confidence is low and wide variance is expected. Below 10 conversions, state "insufficient data for reliable conclusions" and shift reporting to leading indicators (click rate, cost per click, impression share). Source: statistical significance requirements for two-proportion testing applied to PPC performance periods.

**3 paragraphs maximum** for any single report section. No paragraph longer than 3 sentences. No table longer than 6 rows unless showing campaign-level detail. These limits enforce conciseness and prevent the most common report quality failure: restating the same information in progressively vaguer language to fill space. Source: PPC.io report quality standards.

## Common Mistakes and WHY They Happen

**Reporting raw numbers without context.** The report says "$5,000 spent, 42 conversions, $119 CPA." Is that good? Bad? The reader has no idea without knowing the target and the prior period. This happens because extraction is easier than interpretation -- pulling data from Google Ads is mechanical, but adding the "versus target" and "versus last month" comparisons requires the analyst to know the targets and maintain historical context. The discipline is: no metric appears without both comparisons.

**Cherry-picking wins while minimizing problems.** The report leads with a 25% conversion increase and mentions the 40% CPA increase in a subordinate clause on page three. This happens because positive findings are easier to present and the analyst fears the client's reaction to bad news. The discipline is equal treatment -- wins and challenges get the same structural weight, the same level of evidence, and the same specificity in the plan that follows.

**Using PPC jargon in client-facing documents.** "Quality Score improved from 5.2 to 6.8 resulting in lower CPCs and improved IS." Most clients do not know what Quality Score or impression share mean, and asking feels embarrassing. This happens because the analyst thinks in platform terminology and forgets the reader does not. The discipline is translating every term every time, without exception. "Ad relevance rating" instead of Quality Score. "Visibility" instead of impression share. "Cost per new customer" instead of CPA.

**Padding good months to match template length.** The account is performing well, nothing needs attention, but the report template has eight sections, so the analyst manufactures insights to fill them. "Click-through rate remained stable at 4.2%, consistent with the 4-week average" is filler -- it says nothing actionable. This happens because template compliance feels professional and short reports feel lazy. The discipline is: include a section only if the data warrants it. A three-section report with sharp analysis on a strong month is better than an eight-section report with five sections of filler.

**Ending escalation emails without a specific ask.** The email describes a budget pacing problem, explains the impact, outlines options -- and then ends. The client reads it, feels alarmed, and does not know what to do. This happens because the analyst confuses informing with communicating. An escalation without a clear "we need you to decide X by Y date" is bad news without a path forward. The discipline is: every escalation ends with "please confirm by [date] whether you would like us to proceed with [option A] or [option B]."

**Presenting correlation as causation.** "CPA rose during the same period we expanded keywords" becomes "keyword expansion caused the CPA increase." These may be related, but correlation is not causation -- CPA might have risen due to competitive pressure, seasonal shifts, or landing page changes. This happens because the analyst needs an explanation and the timing correlation feels definitive. The discipline is language: "CPA rose during the same period as keyword expansion -- we are investigating whether these are related" is honest. "Keyword expansion caused CPA increase" requires isolation testing to confirm.

## What Changes Over Time

Google's reporting capabilities continue to shift. Performance Max provides significantly less transparency than standard search campaigns, making detailed campaign-level reporting harder for accounts that rely heavily on PMax. Reporting must adapt by focusing on what is measurable (asset group performance, conversion trends, audience signals) rather than pretending granular visibility exists where it does not.

AI-assisted reporting tools are emerging that automate data extraction and basic narrative generation. This shifts the value of human reporting from "what happened" (which AI can summarize) to "what does this mean for your business and what should we do next" (which requires strategic judgment, business context, and relationship knowledge that automation cannot replicate). Reports that only describe metrics will be commoditized. Reports that interpret, diagnose, and recommend will retain their value.

Client expectations for reporting frequency and format are fragmenting. Some clients want comprehensive monthly PDFs. Others want a weekly Slack message. Others want a real-time dashboard they check themselves and only want communication when something is wrong. The reporting format should match the client's consumption preference, not the agency's production preference. Asking "how do you prefer to receive updates?" at the start of an engagement prevents months of mismatched communication.

Attribution complexity continues to increase as privacy regulations reduce tracking precision. Reports must increasingly acknowledge attribution limitations rather than presenting conversion data as absolute truth. "Google Ads reported 42 conversions this month, which may undercount actual impact by 10-15% based on consent rates and cross-device gaps" is more honest than presenting 42 as a hard number.

## Connected Domains

**Account auditing** generates the findings that reports communicate. The audit identifies performance gaps, waste, and opportunities. The report translates those findings into language the client understands and actions they approve. Without auditing rigor, reports have nothing substantive to say. Without reporting quality, audit findings never reach the decision-maker.

**Experimentation** produces results that require careful communication. Experiment outcomes -- especially negative results or trade-offs -- must be presented with statistical context. "The test showed no difference after 200 conversions per arm" is a definitive finding worth communicating. "The test is inconclusive after 30 conversions" requires different framing. Reporting on experiments without communicating statistical confidence misleads the reader about result reliability.

**Competitive intelligence** provides external context that explains internal performance changes. An impression share decline might be a new competitor, not an internal problem. Including competitive context in monthly reports and QBR prep transforms a metric movement from an unexplained data point into a market-aware strategic finding.

**New account onboarding** requires reporting that sets expectations appropriately. First-month reports for new accounts should establish baselines, not force comparisons that do not exist. The reporting tone during onboarding is educational -- helping the client understand what data is being collected, what it will enable, and why patience during the stabilization phase is strategically correct.

**Business model context** determines which metrics matter and how they are framed. Ecommerce clients care about revenue and ROAS. Lead gen clients care about lead volume and cost per qualified lead. SaaS clients care about pipeline value and customer acquisition cost relative to lifetime value. Reporting the wrong primary metric for the business model wastes the client's attention on numbers that do not answer their core question.
