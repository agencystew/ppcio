# PPC Unit Economics Knowledge Foundation

> Unit economics is the math that determines whether acquiring a customer through paid search makes the business money or loses it. Every budget conversation, scaling decision, and campaign verdict depends on this calculation.

## First Principles

**LTV > CAC is the only equation that matters.** Customer Lifetime Value exceeding Customer Acquisition Cost means the business makes money. ROAS, CTR, CVR, CPC, and impression share are signals that help diagnose why the equation is or is not working. They are not goals. A campaign with 1.1x ROAS that acquires customers worth $10,000 in lifetime value at $9,000 acquisition cost is generating $1,000 profit per customer. A campaign with 15x ROAS that generated 2 conversions in 90 days is a statistical artifact, not a business success. The signals point toward the equation. The equation determines reality.

**First-order profitability is the floor, not the ceiling.** The gross profit from a single transaction (AOV multiplied by gross margin) defines the maximum CPA at which you break even on the first purchase. This is the most conservative target and the only safe one when lifetime value data is unvalidated. Optimizing for LTV-based CPA when your LTV estimate comes from industry benchmarks rather than actual cohort data is optimizing for a number you made up. The confidence in your LTV data determines how far above first-order break-even you should be willing to pay.

**Every metric is a ratio, and ratios hide the denominator.** A 5x ROAS campaign spending $1,000/month generates $5,000 in revenue. A 3x ROAS campaign spending $20,000/month generates $60,000. The second campaign is 12x more valuable to the business despite the "worse" ratio. Percentage improvements on small denominators produce tiny dollar outcomes. Dollar thinking, not ratio thinking, drives correct decisions. This is why every unit economics conclusion must be expressed in currency, not percentages. "$12 profit per customer" communicates. "15% of LTV" does not.

**The CPA that Google Ads reports and the CPA your business experiences are different numbers.** Google Ads counts platform-attributed conversions. The business counts actual customers who pay money. The gap between these two numbers (discussed in the Measurement foundation) directly distorts unit economics calculations. Running unit economics on Google Ads data without cross-referencing CRM data is running the most important calculation on the least reliable input.

**Margin is the most underrated variable in PPC.** Two ecommerce stores both selling $100 products. Store A has 60% gross margin ($60 profit per sale), Store B has 20% gross margin ($20 profit per sale). Store A can afford a $50 CPA and still profit. Store B goes bankrupt at the same CPA. Yet both show identical AOV in Google Ads. Margin is invisible to the platform but determines everything about what the business can afford. When margin is unknown, all unit economics conclusions are assumptions.

## The Decision Landscape

**Scale, optimize, or stop?** This is the core decision unit economics answers. If current CPA is more than 20% below the allowable CPA (at your chosen risk level), there is headroom to scale. Increase budgets on the best-performing campaigns. If CPA is within 20% of the allowable threshold, optimize first. Improve conversion rates, reduce waste, tighten targeting. If CPA exceeds the allowable CPA by more than 30%, the campaign is losing money on every acquisition. Pause scaling immediately. The threshold comes from the gap between what you pay and what you can afford, not from any industry benchmark.

**Which allowable CPA threshold to use?** Conservative (25% of LTV) is for cash-constrained, bootstrapped businesses that must stay cash-flow positive on every cohort. Moderate (33% of LTV) balances growth with profitability and works for most established businesses. Aggressive (50% of LTV) requires validated LTV data and available capital, typically growth-funded companies. Land grab (75% of LTV) is venture-backed market capture where proven unit economics justify near-zero-profit acquisition. The threshold selection is a business strategy decision, not a marketing one. It depends on cash reserves, growth mandate, LTV confidence, and competitive pressure.

**First-purchase ROAS or lifetime ROAS?** For one-time purchase businesses (single transaction, no repeat), first-purchase ROAS is the only metric that matters. For repeat-purchase businesses, first-purchase ROAS dramatically understates true return. A subscription SaaS product with $50/month revenue and 2% monthly churn has a $2,500 LTV. Judging that business on first-month ROAS of 0.5x ($50 revenue on $100 CPA) would kill a massively profitable acquisition channel. The decision: use first-purchase ROAS only when LTV equals first-purchase value. Use lifetime-adjusted calculations for everything else, but only with validated LTV data.

**When to accept negative first-order economics.** Losing money on the first transaction is rational only when three conditions hold simultaneously: (1) LTV data from actual customer cohorts (not estimates) shows reliable repeat purchase behavior, (2) the business has sufficient cash to fund the payback period, and (3) the payback period is under 12 months for most businesses or under 18 months for well-capitalized ones. Missing any of these conditions means the first-order loss is not an investment, it is a gamble.

## Industry Branches

### Ecommerce

Ecommerce unit economics are dominated by the ROAS framework because every transaction has a measurable revenue value. The foundational calculation is break-even ROAS, which equals 1 divided by gross margin percentage. A store with 40% margins breaks even at 2.5x ROAS. Below that, every sale loses money. Above it, the margin between actual ROAS and break-even ROAS is profit.

The ecommerce trap is blended ROAS. Brand search campaigns running at 12x ROAS inflate the blended number, making non-brand campaigns at 1.8x ROAS look survivable in aggregate. Separate brand and non-brand immediately. Brand ROAS is not an acquisition metric because those customers were searching for you by name. Non-brand ROAS is the true acquisition efficiency measure. Performance Max further complicates this by blending brand search, Shopping, Display, and YouTube into a single reported ROAS that is almost always inflated by brand traffic.

AOV manipulation is the fastest unit economics lever in ecommerce. A 15% increase in average order value from bundling, free shipping thresholds, or upsells flows directly to the allowable CPA calculation without any change to campaign structure. If AOV rises from $80 to $92 at 40% margin, gross profit per sale rises from $32 to $36.80, expanding the break-even CPA by $4.80 per customer. At 500 conversions per month, that is $2,400/month of additional profit or acquisition headroom from a merchandising change, not a media change.

New versus returning customer separation is critical but frequently ignored. Returning customers who would have purchased anyway show up as Google Ads conversions, inflating ROAS and deflating apparent CPA. If 30% of "conversions" are repeat purchasers, the true new customer acquisition CPA is roughly 40% higher than reported. Google Ads now offers new customer acquisition goals that can partially address this, but the cleanest method is CRM-based deduplication.

### Lead Generation

Lead gen unit economics require a translation layer that most businesses lack. Google Ads reports a cost per lead. The business needs a cost per customer. The bridge is close rate: if 1 in 4 leads becomes a customer, the true CAC is 4x the CPL. A $50 CPL looks efficient until you realize the close rate is 10%, making the true CAC $500. If the average customer value is $400, the business loses $100 on every customer acquired through paid search regardless of how "good" the CPL looks.

The dangerous pattern in lead gen is optimizing for CPL without tracking close rate. Smart Bidding optimizes toward whatever conversion you tell it to target. If you target form submissions, the algorithm finds the cheapest form submissions, which are often the lowest-quality leads. The unit economics only work when the conversion action that Smart Bidding optimizes toward correlates with actual revenue. Offline conversion import (feeding closed-won deals back to Google Ads) is the mechanism that aligns platform optimization with business reality.

Value assignment per lead type is where most lead gen accounts leave money on the table. A "Request a Quote" lead might close at 25% with $2,000 average deal value ($500 expected value per lead). A "Download Whitepaper" lead might close at 2% with $2,000 average deal value ($40 expected value per lead). If both are set to the same conversion value, Smart Bidding treats them equally and chases the cheaper whitepaper downloads. Differentiated conversion values aligned to actual business outcomes are required for unit economics to function in lead gen.

Phone calls add another conversion channel that is frequently either missing from tracking or tracked with insufficient quality filters. A 10-second call from someone who misdialed is counted the same as a 5-minute call from a qualified buyer unless minimum duration thresholds are set (60 seconds is the standard). For service businesses where 40-60% of conversions come through phone calls, missing this data means the unit economics calculation is running on half the picture.

### B2B SaaS (Long Sales Cycle)

B2B SaaS has the most complex unit economics in paid search because of the gap between what happens in Google Ads (click, form fill) and what happens in the business (demo, trial, negotiation, contract, onboarding, expansion, renewal). The CPA that Google Ads reports is a cost per marketing qualified lead. The CAC the business needs to manage includes everything from that click through the sales cycle to a signed contract. Marketing-sourced CAC, fully loaded with sales costs, is the real number.

Churn-based LTV is the defining calculation. LTV equals monthly revenue per customer multiplied by gross margin divided by monthly churn rate. With $200/month ARPU, 80% gross margin, and 3% monthly churn, LTV is ($200 x 0.80) / 0.03 = $5,333. The sensitivity to churn is extreme: change churn to 5% and LTV drops to $3,200, a 40% reduction. This is why SaaS unit economics must include churn sensitivity analysis. A 2-percentage-point churn change can make the difference between a scaling green light and a stop-everything red light.

Payback period dominates SaaS acquisition strategy more than in any other vertical. If the CAC is $3,000 and monthly gross profit per customer is $160, the payback period is 18.75 months. The business must fund 18 months of acquisition cost before recouping a single dollar. At 50 new customers per month, that is $150,000 per month of cash going out the door with 18-month delayed returns. This is why SaaS growth requires either strong cash reserves or venture funding. Unit economics can be positive on paper (LTV of $5,333 vs CAC of $3,000) while the business runs out of cash.

Attribution windows in B2B SaaS must match the sales cycle. If the average time from first click to closed deal is 45 days, a 7-day conversion window captures almost nothing. A 90-day window is appropriate. But longer windows mean more attribution overlap with other channels, making it harder to isolate Google Ads' contribution. The Time Lag report in Google Ads shows the distribution of conversion delay and is essential for setting the right window. Running unit economics on a 30-day attribution window for a product with a 60-day sales cycle systematically understates performance and leads to premature campaign pausing.

### Local Services

Local service unit economics are simpler in structure but harder to track. The average job value multiplied by the close rate equals the expected value per lead. A plumber with $500 average jobs and 30% close rate has $150 expected value per lead. If CPL is $80, the margin per lead is $70. Straightforward. The difficulty is that most local service businesses do not track close rates systematically. They know how many leads they got from Google. They do not know, at the lead level, which ones became jobs.

Geographic CPA variation is a unit economics issue unique to local services. The same plumbing ad costs $15 per click in a small town and $45 per click in a major metro. If the job value and close rate are similar, the metro campaign's unit economics are 3x worse. But if metro jobs average $800 versus $400 for small-town jobs, the math changes. Local service unit economics must be calculated per geography, not blended across the service area.

Seasonality makes local service unit economics volatile. An HVAC company's summer CPA might be $40 (everyone's AC breaks) while winter CPA rises to $120 (fewer searches, more competition). The annual blended CPA might look fine, but if the business makes budget decisions monthly, winter campaigns get paused prematurely because the monthly unit economics look negative. The fix is annualized unit economics with seasonal expectation setting: the business accepts higher CPA in slow months because the annual calculation is profitable.

Repeat and referral value is the hidden LTV for local services. A satisfied plumbing customer calls again for the next issue and refers neighbors. If the average customer generates 2.5 lifetime jobs and 0.5 referral jobs, the true LTV is 3x the single-job value. Most local service businesses have this data informally ("our best customers call us every year") but do not use it to inform acquisition strategy. Even a rough LTV estimate shifts the allowable CPA significantly upward.

### High-Value Verticals (Legal, Medical, Financial)

High-value verticals have the widest gap between CPL and case/client value, making unit economics both enormously profitable and enormously risky. A personal injury law firm pays $200-500 per click. With 3% conversion rate, the CPL is $6,000-16,000. But a single case settlement fee can be $50,000-500,000. The math works if the firm signs enough cases. It fails catastrophically if lead quality is poor or close rates drop.

The case acceptance rate is the critical variable in legal unit economics, analogous to close rate in lead gen but with higher stakes. If 1 in 20 leads becomes a signed case, and average case value is $100,000, each lead is worth $5,000 in expected value. At $8,000 CPL, the firm loses money. At $3,000 CPL, it is highly profitable. The margin between profitable and unprofitable is narrow at these dollar amounts. A 2-percentage-point shift in case acceptance (from 5% to 3%) turns a profitable channel into a cash drain.

Medical and financial verticals face the additional unit economics challenge of long revenue realization timelines. A financial advisor acquiring a client through Google Ads might not see meaningful revenue for 6-12 months as the relationship develops and assets transfer. The LTV is real but the payback period is extremely long. Running unit economics on first-year revenue systematically undervalues these clients. The sophistication required is modeling multi-year revenue per client cohort, which most financial practices do not do.

## Diagnostic Trees

**"My CPA looks good but the business is not growing."** Check whether the "conversions" in Google Ads match actual customers. If Google reports 100 conversions and the CRM shows 40 customers, the real CPA is 2.5x what the platform shows. Next, check whether conversions are new customers or returning ones. If 60% are repeat purchasers, the new customer CPA is dramatically higher than reported. If both check out, the issue may be LTV: customers are being acquired but not retained, meaning the lifetime value is lower than assumed and the CAC is eating the profit. Root cause is one of: tracking inflation, repeat customer miscounting, or LTV shortfall.

**"My ROAS is above target but profitability is declining."** ROAS measures revenue return, not profit return. If product costs or shipping costs increased, margin compressed, and ROAS stayed the same, profitability fell. Alternatively, the campaign mix may have shifted toward lower-margin products. A 5x ROAS on a 15% margin product generates $0.75 profit per dollar spent. A 3x ROAS on a 60% margin product generates $0.80 profit per dollar. The "worse" ROAS is more profitable. Check: are you measuring revenue return or profit return? Has margin changed? Has the product mix shifted?

**"I do not know if I should scale or optimize."** Calculate allowable CPA at moderate threshold (33% of LTV). Compare to current CPA. If current CPA is more than 20% below allowable, scale. Check impression share: if you are losing more than 20% to budget constraints, budget increase is the immediate action. If current CPA is within 20% of allowable, optimize first (waste reduction, conversion rate improvement, negative keywords). If current CPA exceeds allowable by more than 30%, stop scaling and fix fundamentals. The decision is mechanical once you know your allowable CPA and current CPA.

**"I do not know my customer lifetime value."** Start with what you can measure. Pull customer purchase data for the last 12-24 months. Group by first purchase date (cohort). For each cohort, calculate total revenue generated to date. Divide by number of customers in the cohort. That is your observed LTV at 12 or 24 months. If you do not have historical data, use a conservative proxy: assume LTV equals 1.5x first purchase value for businesses with some repeat purchase, and 1.0x for pure one-time purchases. Flag the assumption and update as real data accumulates. Never use aggressive LTV estimates from industry benchmarks to justify high CPA spending.

## Mental Models

**The Allowable CPA Spectrum.** There is not one "correct" CPA for any business. There is a spectrum from first-order break-even (the floor, zero profit) through conservative, moderate, and aggressive thresholds to LTV break-even (the ceiling, zero lifetime profit). Where on this spectrum you choose to operate is a business strategy decision driven by cash position, growth goals, and LTV confidence. The model breaks down when LTV is completely unknown, in which case first-order break-even is the only defensible position.

**The Profitability Hierarchy.** Metrics stack in importance: Conversion Tracking Accuracy > Unit Economics (LTV vs CAC) > Alignment > Efficiency (ROAS, CPA). You cannot calculate unit economics if tracking is broken. You cannot optimize efficiency if unit economics are negative. Each layer depends on the one below it. The model breaks down in early-stage businesses where all layers are simultaneously under construction.

**The ROAS Decomposition.** ROAS equals AOV multiplied by CVR divided by CPC. This means ROAS is not one metric but three levers multiplied together. To improve ROAS, identify which lever has the most headroom. If AOV is 30% below category average, merchandising changes (bundles, upsells) move the number faster than CPC optimization. If CVR is 50% below benchmark, alignment and landing page work are the priority. If CPC is high relative to sustainable levels, Quality Score improvement and keyword refinement help. Diagnose the lever, then apply the fix. "Improve ROAS" is not a diagnosis. The model breaks down when all three levers are simultaneously far from benchmark, which indicates a structural problem beyond lever-by-lever optimization.

**The Payback Period Cash Trap.** Unit economics can be positive on paper while the business runs out of cash. If CAC is $5,000 and monthly gross profit per customer is $200, payback is 25 months. At 30 new customers per month, the business invests $150,000 monthly and does not recoup for over two years. Profitable on a per-customer basis, bankrupt on a cash flow basis. The model highlights that payback period, not just LTV vs CAC, determines viability of acquisition spending.

**Margin as the Invisible Variable.** Two businesses with identical AOV, CPA, and ROAS have completely different profitability if their margins differ. Google Ads cannot see margin. Smart Bidding cannot optimize for it. All platform-side metrics (ROAS, CPA) are margin-blind. The business must impose its margin reality on the platform's optimization targets. Target ROAS must be set based on margin-informed break-even ROAS, not industry benchmarks. The model reinforces that the most important number in unit economics is the one Google Ads never sees.

## Thresholds and Benchmarks

**Break-even ROAS equals 1 divided by gross margin.** At 50% margin, break-even ROAS is 2.0x. At 40% margin, 2.5x. At 25% margin, 4.0x. This is derived from algebra: Revenue multiplied by Margin must equal Cost for break-even, so Revenue/Cost (ROAS) must equal 1/Margin. Every dollar of ROAS above break-even is profit. Every dollar below is loss. This calculation requires knowing your actual gross margin, not an industry estimate.

**Healthy LTV-to-CAC ratio is 3:1 or above.** This comes from SaaS economics research (David Skaggs, Bessemer Venture Partners) and has been widely adopted across digital acquisition. At 3:1, the business retains 67% of customer value after acquisition cost. Below 3:1, the acquisition investment is consuming too large a share of customer value. The exception: early-stage companies in land-grab mode intentionally operate at 1.5-2:1 to capture market share, but this requires funded capital and a plan to improve the ratio through retention.

**Payback period under 12 months is the standard for self-funded businesses.** This threshold comes from cash flow dynamics: a 12-month payback means each customer cohort funds the next cohort's acquisition within one budget cycle. Above 12 months, the business needs external capital or reserves to bridge the gap. SaaS businesses with strong retention can tolerate 12-18 months. Venture-backed businesses may accept 18-24 months. Above 24 months, the compounding cash requirement becomes prohibitive for most business models.

**CPA should not exceed 25-33% of first-order gross profit for conservative businesses.** This preserves 67-75% of first-order margin as profit after acquisition cost, providing a buffer against margin compression, CPA fluctuations, and overhead costs not captured in gross margin. The 25% threshold is for businesses where LTV is unknown or unvalidated. The 33% threshold is for businesses with at least 6 months of cohort data confirming repeat purchase behavior.

**Sensitivity to churn in subscription models: a 1% change in monthly churn changes LTV by 25-50%.** At 2% churn, LTV is based on a 50-month average lifespan. At 3% churn, it drops to 33 months, a 34% reduction. At 5% churn, it falls to 20 months, a 60% reduction from the 2% scenario. This extreme sensitivity is why churn rate is the single most important variable in SaaS unit economics, more impactful than acquisition cost, pricing, or conversion rate. Small churn improvements create outsized LTV gains.

## Common Mistakes and WHY They Happen

**Using industry CPA benchmarks instead of calculating allowable CPA.** "$50 CPA is good for your industry" is meaningless without knowing the business's margins and LTV. A $50 CPA is catastrophic for a business with $40 gross profit per customer and no repeat purchases. It is a gift for a business with $500 LTV. This happens because benchmarks are easy to find and allowable CPA requires internal data (margin, LTV) that marketers often do not have. The fix: calculate your own allowable CPA from your own numbers. If you do not have the numbers, get them from finance before making any budget decisions.

**Optimizing for first-purchase ROAS in a repeat-purchase business.** A subscription box with $30 first-order revenue and $150 CPA looks like a disaster on first-purchase ROAS (0.2x). But if customers stay for 18 months at $30/month, LTV is $540, and the CPA is less than 28% of LTV. Killing this campaign because of first-month ROAS kills a profitable acquisition channel. This mistake happens because first-purchase data is immediately available while LTV takes months to materialize. Patience and cohort tracking are the antidotes.

**Ignoring that PMAX ROAS is inflated by brand traffic.** PMAX reports a blended ROAS that includes brand search traffic (high ROAS, would convert anyway) alongside non-brand discovery traffic (lower ROAS, true acquisition). A reported 6x PMAX ROAS might be 15x brand and 2x non-brand when decomposed. Celebrating the 6x and increasing budget means pouring money into a channel where the true acquisition ROAS is barely profitable. This happens because PMAX is opaque by design. The fix: compare PMAX brand CPA to Search brand CPA. If PMAX is paying more for brand traffic, it is cannibalizing.

**Scaling budget without checking if CPA holds at higher volume.** Current CPA at $5,000/month spend is $45. The manager assumes doubling budget to $10,000 will double conversions at $45 each. In practice, Google Ads operates on an auction curve: the cheapest conversions are captured first, and each marginal conversion costs more. A 100% budget increase typically produces 60-80% more conversions, not 100%. The CPA at $10,000 might be $55. This happens because the CPA-to-volume relationship is not linear, but most planning assumes it is. Scale incrementally (20-30% budget increases) and monitor CPA at each level.

**Treating micro-conversions as equivalent to macro-conversions in value.** A newsletter signup and a demo request both count as "1 conversion" unless differentiated values are assigned. If Smart Bidding treats them equally, it chases the cheapest conversions, which are almost always the lowest-intent actions. This distorts unit economics because the reported CPA blends high-value and low-value actions into a meaningless average. The fix: assign conversion values that reflect actual downstream revenue. A demo request worth $500 and a newsletter signup worth $5 should be valued at 100:1, not 1:1.

**Confusing revenue and profit in ROAS calculations.** A 4x ROAS sounds strong. But if the product margin is 20%, the business makes $0.80 for every $1 spent on ads (4x revenue multiplied by 20% margin = 0.8x profit return). The business is losing $0.20 on every ad dollar. This happens because ROAS is a revenue metric, not a profit metric, and the name "Return on Ad Spend" implies return means profit. It does not. True profit return on ad spend (PROAS) equals ROAS multiplied by margin. A 4x ROAS at 20% margin is equivalent to a 2x ROAS at 40% margin in terms of actual business outcome.

## What Changes Over Time

Google's shift toward value-based bidding means the platform increasingly needs accurate conversion values to optimize correctly. Accounts that provide revenue data, differentiated values by conversion type, and offline conversion import will see better Smart Bidding performance than accounts using flat CPA targets. Unit economics knowledge becomes the input layer for the algorithm, not just a management reporting exercise.

First-party data and server-side tracking are becoming essential for accurate unit economics. As browser cookie restrictions tighten, platform-reported conversions increasingly undercount actual transactions. Businesses that connect CRM data to Google Ads through offline conversion import get more accurate attribution and better Smart Bidding performance. The gap between businesses with strong measurement infrastructure and those without will widen.

The constant: customers either generate more revenue than they cost to acquire, or they do not. The tools for measuring this, the platforms for acquiring customers, and the attribution models for connecting the two will evolve. The fundamental question of whether acquisition spending is profitable remains unchanged.

## Connected Domains

**Alignment Chain** connects upstream. Unit economics tells you whether the output of the alignment chain (conversions) is profitable. A perfectly aligned chain that produces $200 CPA customers when the allowable CPA is $100 has an alignment-healthy but economically-broken campaign. Load together when campaigns convert consistently but at unprofitable levels.

**Measurement** is the input layer. Unit economics calculations are only as reliable as the conversion data they are built on. If Google Ads reports 100 conversions and the CRM shows 55, every unit economics calculation using platform data is wrong by 45%. Load together when platform data and business data diverge.

**Bidding Strategy** is the output layer. Once unit economics defines the allowable CPA or target ROAS, bidding strategy determines how to tell the platform to pursue that target. Target CPA, Target ROAS, and value-based bidding are all mechanisms for translating unit economics into platform instructions. Load together when setting or adjusting bidding strategies.
