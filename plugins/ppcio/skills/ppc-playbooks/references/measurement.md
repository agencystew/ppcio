# Google Ads Measurement Knowledge Foundation

> Measurement is the data layer underneath every PPC decision. When tracking is accurate, optimization works. When tracking is broken, Smart Bidding optimizes toward phantom signals, CPA calculations are wrong, and every dollar decision is built on fiction.

## First Principles

**The CRM is the source of truth, never the platform.** Google Ads reports what it can see: clicks, platform-tracked conversions, and modeled attribution. The business knows what actually happened: leads that answered the phone, deals that closed, revenue that hit the bank account. When the two disagree, the CRM wins. Google Ads data is self-serving by design because the platform benefits from attributing more conversions to itself. GA4 is more neutral but still cookie-dependent. The hierarchy is always: CRM (truth) > GA4 (neutral measurement) > Google Ads (self-attributed).

**Direction matters more than magnitude.** A 20% tracking variance could be under-reporting (Google shows fewer conversions than reality) or over-reporting (Google shows more). These are completely different problems with opposite consequences. Under-reporting means Smart Bidding thinks CPA is higher than it actually is, causing the algorithm to bid conservatively and lose volume. Over-reporting means Smart Bidding thinks CPA is lower than it actually is, causing overbidding on bad traffic. Over-reporting is more dangerous because it actively wastes money. Under-reporting leaves money on the table but does not burn it.

**Tracking accuracy is prerequisite, not optional.** Before optimizing bids, testing ads, adjusting budgets, or restructuring campaigns, validate that conversions are being measured correctly. A 40% tracking discrepancy makes every subsequent optimization decision unreliable. If Google Ads shows 100 conversions but the CRM shows 60, the "real" CPA is 67% higher than reported. Smart Bidding targets, budget allocations, and performance verdicts built on the platform number are all systematically wrong.

**Smart Bidding amplifies tracking errors.** Manual bidding with broken tracking means you overpay or underpay for clicks. Smart Bidding with broken tracking means the machine learning algorithm actively optimizes toward the wrong signals. If phantom conversions inflate the conversion count, Smart Bidding learns to bid aggressively on the traffic patterns that produce phantom conversions. The algorithm becomes confidently wrong. This is why broken tracking plus Smart Bidding is the most expensive combination in Google Ads, worse than broken tracking with manual bids.

**Every conversion action in the account has settings that affect what Smart Bidding sees.** The counting method (One vs Every), the attribution model (Last Click vs Data-Driven), the conversion window (7 to 90 days), the include-in-conversions toggle, and the conversion value all shape the data that Smart Bidding uses to make bidding decisions. A single misconfigured setting, like a lead form set to "Every" counting instead of "One," can double the apparent conversion count and halve the apparent CPA, causing the algorithm to overbid systematically.

## The Decision Landscape

**3-way validation: when and why.** Comparing Google Ads conversions, GA4 conversions, and CRM actual results for the same time period reveals both the magnitude and likely cause of discrepancies. If Google Ads and GA4 both show 100 but CRM shows 60, the tracking infrastructure is probably fine but the attribution is generous (counting assisted conversions, view-throughs, or cross-device). If Google Ads shows 100, GA4 shows 70, and CRM shows 65, GA4 is closer to reality and the gap between Google Ads and GA4 points to platform over-attribution. If all three numbers are wildly different, the tagging infrastructure itself is likely broken.

**Can you trust Smart Bidding right now?** If tracking variance is below 15%, Smart Bidding has clean enough data to optimize effectively. Between 15-25%, proceed with caution and fix tracking while monitoring. Between 25-50%, consider switching to manual CPC or Maximize Clicks until tracking is fixed, because Smart Bidding is working with corrupted signal. Above 50%, stop Smart Bidding immediately. The algorithm is optimizing toward garbage. These thresholds come from the practical reality that Smart Bidding can tolerate some noise (all measurement has variance), but beyond 25%, the signal-to-noise ratio degrades optimization quality measurably.

**Fix today vs fix this month.** Critical tracking fixes (duplicate tags firing, phantom conversions inflating data, primary conversion action broken) require same-day action because every hour they persist, Smart Bidding learns from bad data. High-priority fixes (Enhanced Conversions not enabled, conversion window mismatched to sales cycle) should be addressed within a week. Medium-priority improvements (switching from Last Click to Data-Driven attribution, adding offline conversion import) improve optimization quality but do not cause active damage while waiting.

**When to re-enable Smart Bidding after a tracking fix.** The algorithm needs a clean data period to recalibrate. Wait for at least 14 consecutive days of tracking variance under 15%, a minimum of 30 conversions in that clean period, and no additional tracking changes during those 14 days. Rushing back to Smart Bidding before the data is clean forces the algorithm to learn from a mixture of corrupted and clean data, producing unreliable optimization.

## Industry Branches

### Ecommerce

Ecommerce measurement is structurally simpler than other verticals because the conversion event (purchase) has a definitive value (transaction revenue) that happens in a trackable digital environment. The primary measurement challenges are dynamic value capture, cross-domain tracking, and returning customer attribution.

Dynamic conversion values must pass the actual transaction amount to Google Ads, not a static default value. If the data layer sends the wrong revenue number (excluding tax when it should include it, or reporting the discounted price instead of pre-discount), ROAS calculations are systematically wrong. A 10% error in transaction value reporting means Target ROAS bidding operates 10% off-target on every conversion. For a store processing 500 orders per month through Google Ads, a $5 average value error represents $2,500 per month of phantom revenue that Smart Bidding treats as real.

Cross-domain tracking breaks are common in ecommerce because checkout often happens on a different domain or subdomain (Shopify checkout, payment processor redirects, third-party cart systems). When the tracking cookie does not persist across the domain boundary, the conversion is attributed to "direct" traffic instead of the Google Ads click. The result is systematic under-reporting of paid conversions. The fix is cross-domain linker configuration in GA4 and ensuring the Google Ads tag persists through the full purchase path.

New vs returning customer measurement is essential for accurate acquisition economics but rarely implemented correctly. Google's new customer acquisition goal helps but requires a customer list upload or conversion tag modification. Without it, returning customers who click a Google Ad and purchase appear as new conversions, inflating acquisition ROAS. For stores where 25-40% of Google Ads "conversions" are repeat purchasers, the true acquisition CPA is 30-60% higher than reported.

### Lead Generation

Lead gen measurement has a fundamental gap: the thing Google Ads counts (form submission, phone call) is not the thing the business values (qualified lead, closed deal). Every lead gen measurement strategy must bridge this gap or accept that optimization is happening on a proxy metric that may or may not correlate with revenue.

The counting method is the most frequently misconfigured setting in lead gen accounts. "Every" counting means each time a person submits a form, it counts as a new conversion. A prospect who submits the same form three times (confirmation page reload, re-submission, multiple inquiries) generates three conversions in Google Ads but represents one actual lead. For lead gen, "One" counting (one conversion per click) is correct in almost all cases. This single setting error can double or triple reported conversions, halving or thirding the apparent CPA and causing Smart Bidding to dramatically overbid.

Offline conversion import is the mechanism that closes the measurement gap for lead gen. The process captures the GCLID (Google Click Identifier) at form submission, stores it in the CRM alongside the lead, then feeds back the downstream outcome (qualified, opportunity created, deal won) to Google Ads. This gives Smart Bidding actual business outcomes to optimize toward rather than form fills. Accounts using offline conversion import consistently outperform those optimizing for form submissions because the algorithm learns which click patterns produce revenue, not just form completions.

Phone call measurement requires specific duration thresholds to separate signal from noise. A 5-second call is a misdial. A 15-second call might be a qualification screening. A 60-second call is a genuine inquiry. The standard minimum duration for counting a phone call as a conversion is 60 seconds. Setting it lower inflates conversion counts with low-quality calls. Setting it higher misses legitimate short conversions (scheduling an appointment takes 45 seconds). For businesses where phone calls represent 30-60% of total conversions, getting this threshold right directly impacts Smart Bidding's optimization accuracy.

### B2B SaaS (Long Sales Cycle)

B2B SaaS measurement is defined by the gap between digital conversion events and actual revenue. The digital conversion (demo request, trial signup, content download) happens weeks or months before the business outcome (contract signed). The conversion window setting in Google Ads determines how long the platform waits before attributing a conversion to a click. If the average sales cycle is 45 days and the conversion window is 30 days, the platform misses every conversion that takes longer than 30 days to close. For B2B SaaS, 90-day click-through windows are standard.

The Time Lag report is critical for B2B measurement calibration. This report shows the distribution of conversion delay, revealing that a significant percentage of conversions happen 30, 45, or 60+ days after the initial click. Judging a B2B campaign on 30-day performance is like judging a crop after 2 weeks of growth. The data is not wrong; it is incomplete. Recent weeks always look worse than they actually are because conversions are still in the pipeline. Experienced B2B advertisers discount the most recent 30-45 days of data and make decisions on fully matured cohorts.

Micro-conversion pollution is particularly acute in B2B SaaS. The temptation to add content downloads, pricing page views, video watches, and chatbot interactions as primary conversions "to give Smart Bidding more data" pollutes the conversion signal. If a demo request is worth $500 in expected pipeline value and a whitepaper download is worth $10, treating them as equivalent (both count as "1 conversion") teaches Smart Bidding that cheap whitepaper clickers are just as valuable as qualified demo requesters. The fix: either use differentiated conversion values (50:1 ratio) or exclude micro-conversions from the primary conversion set entirely and track them as observation-only secondary conversions.

Multi-touch attribution is inherently more complex in B2B because the buyer journey involves multiple people, multiple channels, and multiple sessions over weeks or months. A CFO might see a Display ad, the Director of IT might search for the product and click a Search ad, and the procurement team might search the brand name and convert through a Brand campaign. Google Ads attributes the conversion to the last (or data-driven modeled) click, which misrepresents the contribution of the Display awareness campaign. This is a measurement limitation, not a fixable error. Acknowledge it when interpreting B2B performance data.

### Local Services

Local service measurement revolves around phone calls and form fills, with phone calls typically representing the majority of conversions. The challenge is that phone calls are harder to track accurately than digital form submissions. Call tracking requires either Google forwarding numbers (limited in some regions), third-party call tracking platforms (CallRail, CallTrackingMetrics), or manual call logging. Each method has different accuracy characteristics.

Google forwarding numbers work well for Google Ads attribution but do not track calls from organic listings or other channels. Third-party call tracking provides cross-channel visibility but introduces additional tracking points that can fail. Manual logging is the least accurate but sometimes the only option for small businesses. The measurement strategy for local services must explicitly define how phone calls are tracked and what quality threshold (call duration) separates real inquiries from noise.

After-hours and weekend calls create a measurement gap for local services. If the business closes at 5 PM but the ad runs until midnight, clicks after hours generate calls to voicemail. Some of those callers leave messages and become customers. Others call a competitor instead. The conversion tracking may count the call but the business never converts it. This shows up as inflated conversion counts with lower-than-expected close rates. The fix is either adjusting ad schedules to match business hours or ensuring after-hours call handling (answering service, callback system) converts the off-hours intent.

Geographic attribution in local services often breaks when a customer searches in one location but the job site is in another. A plumber serving a 30-mile radius gets a call from someone in City A requesting service at a rental property in City B. Google Ads attributes the conversion to City A (where the click happened). The business experiences the revenue in City B (where the work happens). For businesses making geographic budget decisions, this mismatch can lead to over-investing in areas that generate clicks but under-investing in areas that generate revenue.

### High-Value Verticals (Legal, Medical, Financial)

High-value vertical measurement faces the paradox that each conversion is worth enormous revenue, but the path from click to revenue is long, complex, and difficult to track digitally. A personal injury firm spends $300 per click. The click produces a phone call. The call is screened by intake. The lead is evaluated by an attorney. A case is accepted or rejected. If accepted, the case takes 12-36 months to settle. The firm receives a percentage of the settlement. Connecting the $300 click to the $50,000 fee requires measurement infrastructure that most firms do not have.

Call quality scoring is essential in high-value verticals because the cost per call is so high that even small quality improvements have outsized dollar impact. At $500 per call (assuming $300 CPC and 60% call conversion rate from landing page), the difference between a 10% intake acceptance rate and a 15% rate is the difference between $5,000 and $3,333 per accepted case in acquisition cost. Call tracking platforms that record, transcribe, and score calls enable quality analysis that raw conversion counting cannot provide.

HIPAA, attorney-client privilege, and financial regulations add compliance layers to measurement in these verticals. Medical practices cannot pass patient health information through conversion tracking tags. Law firms must protect client identity in analytics data. Financial institutions face data handling requirements that constrain what information flows through tracking pixels. The measurement system must be designed with these constraints from the start, not retrofitted after a compliance violation.

Conversion value assignment in high-value verticals requires case-type or service-type differentiation. A bankruptcy consultation worth $3,000 and a personal injury case worth $100,000 should not share the same conversion value. If the ad account serves multiple practice areas or service lines, each conversion action should carry a value reflecting the average case or engagement value for that specific type. Without this differentiation, Smart Bidding optimizes for the cheapest leads regardless of their downstream value.

## Diagnostic Trees

**"Google Ads shows more conversions than my CRM."** This is over-reporting, the more dangerous direction. Check first for duplicate tags: view the page source on the conversion page and count tracking scripts. If both a hard-coded Google Ads tag and a GTM-deployed tag fire on the same page, every conversion is double-counted. Second, check the counting method. If set to "Every" for a lead form, repeated submissions from the same person count as separate conversions. Third, check if non-primary conversions (page views, newsletter signups, chat opens) are included in the "Conversions" column. Each of these inflates the count and makes the CPA appear lower than reality. Fix by eliminating duplicates, setting lead forms to "One" counting, and moving non-primary actions to observation only.

**"Google Ads shows fewer conversions than my CRM."** This is under-reporting. Check for tag firing failures: has the thank you page URL changed, breaking the trigger? Has a GTM container been updated with a new trigger rule that excludes the conversion page? Check for cross-domain tracking gaps: does the conversion path cross to a different domain (checkout processor, booking system) where the tracking cookie is lost? Check if Enhanced Conversions is enabled. Without it, 15-30% of conversions may be lost to cookie blocking and privacy restrictions. Check the conversion window: if the window is 7 days but your average conversion delay is 14 days, half the conversions fall outside the attribution window.

**"My Smart Bidding performance suddenly degraded."** Check for recent tracking changes first. A new GTM version, an updated tag, a changed conversion action, or a modified conversion value can all disrupt Smart Bidding by changing the data it learns from. The algorithm interprets a sudden drop in conversions as a signal that previous bid levels were too aggressive, and it reduces bids. A sudden spike (from a duplicate tag) triggers aggressive bidding on junk signals. Check the change history in the Google Ads account for any conversion action modifications in the past 2-3 weeks. Also check whether Enhanced Conversions was recently enabled or disabled, as this changes the volume of reported conversions.

**"My conversion numbers do not match between Google Ads and GA4."** Attribution model differences are the most common cause. Google Ads uses its own attribution (often data-driven, click-based), while GA4 uses a separate data-driven model that includes organic and other channels. A conversion attributed to Google Ads by the Ads platform might be attributed to organic search by GA4 if the user's last interaction before converting was an organic click. Additionally, GA4 uses different counting windows and deduplication logic. A 10-20% variance between Google Ads and GA4 is normal and does not indicate a tracking error. Above 20%, investigate whether tags are firing differently or whether conversion actions are configured inconsistently between platforms.

## Mental Models

**The 3-Way Validation Triangle.** Always compare three data sources: platform (Google Ads), analytics (GA4), and business reality (CRM). Two sources agreeing while one diverges points to the problem. All three diverging points to a fundamental tagging issue. All three agreeing within 10% means tracking is healthy. This model breaks down when the business has no CRM data, reducing the triangle to a two-point comparison with no ground truth.

**Garbage In, Garbage Out, Amplified.** Manual bidding with bad data wastes money at a linear rate: you overpay on clicks at whatever wrong level you set. Smart Bidding with bad data wastes money at an accelerating rate: the algorithm learns from the wrong signal, optimizes toward it, and compounds the error with each bidding cycle. The worse the tracking data, the more damage Smart Bidding inflicts relative to manual bidding. This model explains why fixing tracking is more urgent for accounts using Smart Bidding than for accounts with manual bids.

**The Phantom Conversion Tax.** Over-reported conversions act as a hidden tax on actual conversions. If Google Ads reports 200 conversions but only 100 are real, and the account spends $10,000, the reported CPA is $50 but the actual CPA is $100. Smart Bidding targets $50 CPA and bids accordingly, acquiring a mix of real conversions and phantom ones at $50 each. Since phantom conversions do not generate revenue, the business effectively pays $100 for each real customer while the platform reports $50. The tax is invisible until you cross-reference with CRM data.

**The Attribution Window as a Lens.** The conversion window does not change reality. It changes what the platform can see. A 7-day window for a product with a 30-day sales cycle does not mean conversions after day 7 did not happen; it means Google Ads does not get credit for them, Smart Bidding does not learn from them, and the reported CPA looks higher than it actually is. Changing the window does not create or destroy conversions. It adjusts the lens through which the platform observes them. Set the lens to match reality.

**Primary vs Secondary as a Volume Dial.** The "Include in Conversions" toggle is a volume dial for Smart Bidding. Primary conversions (included) are what the algorithm optimizes toward. Secondary conversions (excluded) are tracked for reporting but invisible to the bidding algorithm. Moving a low-value action from primary to secondary does not delete data. It removes noise from the optimization signal. The model helps decide which actions deserve primary status: only those that directly correlate with business revenue at consistent value.

## Thresholds and Benchmarks

**0-10% variance between Google Ads and CRM is acceptable.** This comes from normal attribution timing differences (conversions credited to different days based on click vs conversion timestamp), minor cookie loss, and cross-device behavior that the platform models but the CRM does not. Within this band, tracking is healthy. No corrective action needed.

**10-25% variance requires investigation.** This band is too large to ignore but not necessarily broken. Common causes: Enhanced Conversions not enabled (causing 15-20% cookie-based loss), conversion window shorter than actual sales cycle (cutting off late conversions), or view-through conversions adding non-click-based attributions. Each cause has a specific fix. Diagnose direction first (over or under), then match to likely cause.

**Above 25% variance means tracking is materially broken.** At this level, CPA and ROAS calculations are wrong enough to cause bad decisions. A campaign that appears profitable at reported CPA might be unprofitable at actual CPA. Budget decisions, campaign verdicts, and scaling recommendations based on this data are unreliable. Fix tracking before making any optimization changes.

**Above 50% variance is a crisis.** Smart Bidding is operating essentially blind. The algorithm's model of account performance is wrong by more than half. Every bidding decision the platform makes is based on a fundamentally incorrect picture. Stop Smart Bidding, switch to manual or Maximize Clicks, fix tracking, accumulate 14 days of clean data with 30 or more conversions, then re-enable target-based bidding.

**Enhanced Conversions recovers 5-15% of otherwise lost conversions.** This range comes from Google's published data and practitioner testing. The recovery rate depends on the proportion of traffic from browsers with strict cookie policies (Safari, Firefox), the prevalence of ad blockers in your audience, and whether your conversion page captures hashed first-party data (email, phone). For B2C audiences with high mobile Safari usage, the recovery is closer to 15%. For B2B desktop Chrome audiences, closer to 5%.

**Lead gen counting method: "One" for leads, "Every" for purchases.** This is derived from the nature of the conversion event. A person submitting a lead form twice is one lead. A person buying two products is two purchases. Mis-setting this is one of the top 3 most common tracking errors, found in over 30% of accounts audited. The impact is binary: "Every" on lead forms roughly doubles the reported conversion count, halving the apparent CPA and causing Smart Bidding to overbid by approximately 2x.

**Minimum 30 conversions per campaign per month for Smart Bidding to function.** This comes from Google's own recommendations and practical observation. Below 30 conversions, the algorithm does not have enough signal to distinguish good traffic patterns from random noise. Performance is erratic and often worse than manual bidding. Below 15 conversions, Smart Bidding is essentially guessing. The solution for low-volume accounts is either campaign consolidation (fewer campaigns with more conversions each) or using a higher-funnel conversion action (qualified lead instead of closed deal) to increase volume, then layering offline conversion import to maintain quality signal.

## Common Mistakes and WHY They Happen

**Trusting platform data over CRM data when they disagree.** This happens because Google Ads data is immediately available, neatly formatted, and updated daily. CRM data requires export, reconciliation, and often manual comparison. The convenience of platform data creates a gravitational pull toward accepting it as truth. The problem: Google Ads benefits from reporting more conversions (it justifies more ad spend), making it structurally biased toward over-attribution. Always use the harder-to-access CRM data as the anchor.

**Adding micro-conversions as primary actions to "give Smart Bidding more data."** This feels logical because Google recommends 30 or more conversions per campaign and low-volume accounts struggle to hit that threshold. Adding page views or scroll depth as conversions generates volume. But it dilutes the signal. Smart Bidding treats all primary conversions equally unless values are differentiated. A "viewed pricing page" conversion mixed with "submitted demo request" teaches the algorithm that pricing page viewers are equally valuable prospects. The fix: use micro-conversions as secondary (observation only) or assign appropriately low values (e.g., $1 vs $500 for a demo request).

**Not checking conversion counting method after account setup.** The counting method is set once during conversion action creation and almost never revisited. If someone created the lead form conversion and selected "Every" (which is the default for some action types), it stays that way for months or years. Nobody checks because the CPA looks "normal" based on the inflated numbers. This is one of the most common and most damaging configuration errors, found in roughly 30% of lead gen accounts. A 30-second settings check prevents years of bidding distortion.

**Assuming 10-15% tracking variance is "close enough."** For small accounts spending $2,000/month, 15% variance means $300/month of mismeasured spend, which is manageable. For accounts spending $200,000/month, 15% variance means $30,000/month of decisions made on incorrect data. The tolerance for variance should scale with spend, not with percentage. At high spend, even 10% variance can mean five-figure monthly dollar impact on optimization decisions.

**Running offline conversion import once and never maintaining it.** Offline conversion import requires ongoing data flow: every lead captured must have its GCLID stored, and every downstream outcome (qualified, opportunity, won, lost) must be fed back to Google Ads within the attribution window. If the CRM integration breaks, the feedback loop stops, and Smart Bidding reverts to optimizing on front-end form submissions only. The system requires monitoring, not just initial setup. A monthly check that the GCLID capture rate, match rate, and import volume are consistent prevents silent degradation.

**Ignoring view-through conversions inflating Search performance.** View-through conversions (a user sees a Display or YouTube ad, then later searches and converts through Search) are sometimes included in Search campaign reporting. This makes Search CPA look lower than it actually is because conversions influenced by other channels are attributed to Search. For Search-only campaigns, view-through attribution should typically be set to 1 day or disabled entirely. Leaving it at the default (depending on campaign type) can add 5-15% phantom conversions to Search reporting.

## What Changes Over Time

Privacy regulations and browser restrictions are eroding cookie-based tracking. Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention, Firefox's Enhanced Tracking Protection, and Chrome's evolving Privacy Sandbox mean that a decreasing percentage of user journeys can be tracked through traditional cookies. Enhanced Conversions (hashed first-party data), server-side tagging, and consent-based measurement frameworks are becoming necessary to maintain tracking accuracy. Accounts that do not adopt these technologies will see increasing under-reporting over time.

Google's Consent Mode and modeled conversions attempt to fill the gap by estimating conversions that cannot be directly observed. This introduces a new variable: modeled conversions are statistical estimates, not observed events. They improve overall measurement accuracy but add a layer of uncertainty. Accounts in regions with strict consent requirements (EU under GDPR) may see 20-40% of conversions modeled rather than observed. Understanding the proportion of modeled conversions in your data is increasingly important for accurate unit economics.

Offline conversion import and first-party data integration are moving from "nice to have" to "required for competitive performance." Accounts that feed closed-won data back to Google Ads give the algorithm a richer optimization signal than accounts relying solely on front-end conversions. As more advertisers adopt this, the competitive baseline shifts. Accounts without offline conversion import will find their Smart Bidding performance falling behind peers who provide stronger signals.

The constants: the need to measure what actually happened in the business, not just what the platform reports. The need to validate that tracking infrastructure works before optimizing on the data it produces. The fundamental principle that decisions are only as good as the data they are based on. These hold regardless of which specific tracking technology or privacy framework is current.

## Connected Domains

**Alignment Chain** depends on measurement to diagnose. The alignment chain diagnostic uses CTR, CVR, bounce rate, and Quality Score as signals. If conversion tracking is broken, CVR is wrong, and the alignment diagnosis may point to the wrong link. Load together when alignment symptoms seem contradictory or when metrics do not match observable behavior.

**Unit Economics** is built on measurement outputs. Every CPA, ROAS, and LTV calculation uses conversion data as an input. If the input is wrong, the economics are wrong. A campaign that looks profitable on platform data but unprofitable on CRM data has a measurement problem, not a profitability problem. Load together when the unit economics verdict does not match business-level financial results.

**Bidding Strategy** translates measurement into action. Smart Bidding uses the conversion data that measurement provides to make bid decisions in real time. The quality of bidding is bounded by the quality of measurement. Load together when Smart Bidding performance is erratic, degrading, or does not match expected behavior given the account structure and targeting.
